A resident clears a communication station, the rubric returns a number, and the program files it. Nobody in the room believes the number settles what that resident will do at two in the morning with a family that is frightened and angry. The useful question is not whether to trust the score. It is how far the score reaches, and the published evidence answers that more precisely than most programs realize.

## What an OSCE score is evidence of

George Miller set out four levels of assessment in 1990: knows, knows how, shows how, and does. An OSCE sits at shows how. The learner demonstrates a skill in a room built to let them demonstrate it, with a trained standardized patient, a scripted opening, and a rubric written in advance. The ward is the does level, where nothing has been arranged and the opportunity to show a behavior may never arrive.

That difference is the point of the station rather than a flaw in it. A well built case [creates an opening for every scored behavior](/insights/osce-case-design-guide), because a program can't score what the encounter never gave the learner a chance to say. The cost of that design is that the station reports performance under favorable conditions.

## The score does carry forward

The strongest evidence that a clinical skills score reaches past the exam room comes from a Canadian cohort. Tamblyn and colleagues followed 3,424 physicians who took the Medical Council of Canada clinical skills examination between 1993 and 1996 and went on to practice in Ontario, Quebec, or both. They tracked complaints to the provincial regulators through 2005, covering two to twelve years of early practice, and published the result in JAMA in 2007.

Over that period, 1,116 complaints were filed and 696 were retained after investigation. Of the physicians, 17.1 percent had at least one retained complaint, and 81.9 percent of those retained complaints were for communication or quality of care. A 2 standard deviation decrease in the communication score was associated with 1.17 more retained complaints per 100 physicians per year (relative risk 1.38, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.61) and 1.20 more communication complaints per 100 practice-years (relative risk 1.43, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.77).

A score recorded in a standardized room in 1994 still said something about how a physician treated patients a decade later. That is a stronger result than the usual defense of simulation claims, and it deserves to be quoted accurately.

## How much it carries

The same paper sets the limit. After adjusting for what the written examination's clinical decision-making score already predicted, communication scores in the bottom quartile explained an additional 9.2 percent (95% CI 4.7 percent to 13.1 percent) of complaints. The relationship held (likelihood ratio test, p&lt;.001), and it still accounts for a small slice of who ends up with a complaint.

Both halves matter. A program that treats a low station score as noise is ignoring a signal that survived twelve years of practice in a cohort of more than three thousand physicians. A program that treats a low station score as a prediction about the resident sitting in front of them is reading a population finding as a personal one, which the data doesn't support. Wenghofer and Boulet reviewed three decades of this literature in 2022 and concluded that examination performance predicts later physician behavior and that the relationship didn't weaken with practice experience. They also wrote that the validity argument is never complete.

## Not every OSCE score is the same score

Tamblyn's number came from a national examination with a published blueprint, trained raters, and many stations. A four station local OSCE graded by two faculty members on a Friday afternoon is a different instrument wearing the same name, and the reliability literature shows how far apart the two can sit.

A 2025 meta-analysis pooled 26 reliability coefficients from 23 OSCE studies and found Cronbach's alpha ranging from 0.43 to 0.93, with 11 of the 23 reaching the 0.8 threshold for good internal consistency. Designs using 5 to 10 stations of under 10 minutes each performed best, at 0.88 (95% CI 0.86 to 1.00). Internal consistency isn't the same thing as predictive validity, and a score that isn't reliable can't predict anything at all. Before a program asks what its OSCE score forecasts, it should be able to say what its own alpha is.

## The ward record is also a sample

The comparison people reach for is a clean exam number against rich clinical observation. The clinical observation is thinner than that framing suggests.

Diller and colleagues coded 2,517 workplace-based narrative assessments from one emergency medicine residency across an academic year. Each narrative identified an average of 2.04 sub-competencies, and 14 of the 23 sub-competencies appeared in fewer than 5 percent of assessments. Patient-centered communication was among the most frequently identified, which is better news for communication than for procedural skills, and it still means the ward record is built from short, uneven glimpses rather than sustained observation.

So the program isn't choosing between a sample and the truth. It is holding two samples of different kinds, each with a known weakness, and deciding how many of each it needs.

## More occasions beats a better single occasion

ACGME's own guidance on the harmonized interpersonal and communication skills milestones says that performance in this domain "requires direct observations of skills in the patient care setting, with scenarios, or using simulation," and names 360-degree evaluation and longitudinal methods alongside those. What those methods have in common is repetition across settings and observers rather than a search for the one assessment that settles the question.

Three things follow for a clerkship director or program director, and all three are work a program can do without buying anything.

Write down what a passing score will allow the program to conclude, before the station runs. A score with no stated meaning can't be over-read or under-read, because nobody agreed what reading it would be.

Calculate and report the reliability of your own OSCE. If the figure is closer to 0.43 than 0.93, that is the first problem to fix, and it is a design problem rather than a rater problem.

Count the separate occasions on which each learner's communication is observed by someone in a year. Programs usually find the number smaller than they expected, and that count, more than any single score, decides what the program actually knows.

## Where practice fits

With ClinicalSim, participants practice spoken conversations with AI patients and receive feedback scored against a named rubric, with transcript evidence they and faculty can inspect. What that adds to a program is occasions. Those scores are formative evidence for faculty review and don't replace human judgment, a station a program has built, or a standardized patient encounter.

The argument of this post applies to our own output as well. One ClinicalSim encounter is a sample taken under arranged conditions, the same as one OSCE station. Programs can read [complete example encounters](/examples) with recordings, transcripts, and reports before deciding what the output is worth, and our [methodology](/methodology) sets out the scoring and its limits. What repeated practice changes is how many samples a program holds when a committee has to make a decision, which is the part [no single assessment has ever carried on its own](/insights/what-programs-lost-when-step-2-cs-disappeared).
