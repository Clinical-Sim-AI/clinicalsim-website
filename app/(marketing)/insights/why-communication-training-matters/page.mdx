Communication factors appear in a large share of asserted malpractice cases, and several training studies report gains in knowledge or simulated performance. Those findings do not show that one training product will improve every outcome.

This page separates the questions that often get bundled together: where communication appears in published risk data, what training studies measured, and what a program can reasonably track.

## Malpractice data describes exposure, not product impact

Candello found communication factors in 40% of asserted malpractice cases from 2014 through 2024. The report describes patterns in claims data. It does not show that a particular course, simulation method, or product would have prevented those claims.

Hickson and colleagues found that malpractice claims clustered among a small group of physicians and that unsolicited patient complaints helped identify higher-risk physicians. That is an association in the study population, not proof that communication alone caused a claim.

[Read how to build a communication training business case without inventing an ROI](/insights/hospital-communication-training-roi).

## Training outcomes depend on the intervention and measure

Communication training studies do not all test the same intervention. A lecture may measure knowledge. A workshop may measure confidence. A simulation may measure observed behavior in a simulated encounter. A multi-part safety program may combine training with process and policy changes.

Results should stay attached to that design. Improvement in a simulated encounter does not establish an effect on patient outcomes, HCAHPS, readmissions, or malpractice claims.

[Review the evidence for repeated breaking bad news practice](/insights/breaking-bad-news-practice-not-knowledge).

## Programs can measure what training is designed to change

A communication curriculum can track participation, repeated attempts, rubric scores, transcript evidence, and faculty review. Programs can also define local measures before launch and decide who can interpret them.

ClinicalSim scores simulated conversations against published frameworks or the institution's own rubric and quotes the participant's own words under every score, by person and by cohort. The report is one source of formative evidence, and faculty retain authority over coaching, remediation, progression, and entrustment decisions.
