## The cost is a set of local inputs, not a national price tag

There is no defensible US headline price for a standardized patient encounter. Programs pay different actor wages and use different amounts of training, faculty time, space, and administrative support, so a per-encounter range without those components is not comparable across institutions.

Bosse and colleagues made that distinction explicit in a study of fifth-year medical students. The researchers counted man-hours rather than inventing a universal dollar figure. Their standardized patient group required 172 man-hours compared with 112 for the peer role-play group, a 53.6% difference in that program. The study did not test AI patients, and its costs should not be generalized beyond its design.

## Standardized patients and on-demand practice have different jobs

Live standardized patients bring human judgment and physical presence to an encounter. Those qualities matter in OSCEs, summative assessment, and complex scenarios where the learner's behavior in the room is part of the skill being assessed.

On-demand AI patient practice addresses a narrower problem: repetition between scheduled encounters. A learner can retry the same conversation, review feedback tied to the transcript, and return to faculty or an SP encounter with specific behaviors to work on.

## Extend the SP program, don't replace it

The strongest program uses each method where it fits. AI patients can carry repeatable voice-based practice, while standardized patients and faculty remain responsible for the high-stakes assessments and coaching that require people in the room.

The useful comparison is not a fabricated cost range versus a software price. It is whether the program has enough practice opportunities before learners reach the limited encounters where human time matters most.
