Researchers from CUHK Shenzhen, Nankai University, and the MIT Media Lab interviewed 12 clinical-year medical students and ran three codesign workshops. The students named six requirements for an AI patient they would use. Their answers put feedback, case quality, and faculty involvement ahead of novelty.

The sample is small and qualitative, so it should guide design questions rather than stand in for the preferences of all medical students. The authors state the headline finding plainly:

> "Our findings position AI-SPs as tools for deliberate practice and show that instructional usability, rather than conversational realism alone, drives learner trust, engagement, and educational value."
>
> Gao Z, Zhu G, Luo H, et al., Extended Abstracts of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, 2026

## Learners want the mode to match the goal

Students wanted different modes for exam preparation, open practice, and scaffolded skill development. A single encounter design cannot serve each job equally well.

The program should tell learners whether they are exploring, practicing against a standard, or completing an assessment. Difficulty and feedback can then match that purpose.

## Information rules need to be clear

Students wanted visible findings to appear without a special prompt and other information to follow appropriate clinical questions. They also wanted those rules to stay consistent across encounters.

That is a case design issue, not just a language model issue. The patient should reveal information because of the clinical interaction, not because the learner guessed the right phrase.

## Voice needs a fallback

Students expected voice to fail sometimes and asked for a text fallback, keyword shortcuts, and hints. Accessibility and recovery matter because a technical error should not become evidence about clinical performance.

## Learners want control over difficulty and feedback

The workshops produced requests for adjustable challenge, limited cues during an encounter, structured review afterward, and links from errors to learning resources. These requests frame AI patients as practice tools rather than automatic judges.

## Patient variation should serve a learning objective

Students wanted to practice with different ages, emotional responses, cultural backgrounds, and health beliefs. Variation is useful when it changes the behavior a learner must demonstrate. Random novelty can make cases less comparable without improving the lesson.

## Faculty involvement still matters

The researchers positioned AI patients as a complement to human standardized patients. AI patients can add repeatable practice and configurable cases. Standardized patients and faculty remain important for embodied interaction, live coaching, and high-stakes assessment.

For ClinicalSim, the practical test is straightforward: does each case state its objective, use a named framework, and give the learner feedback that a faculty member can inspect?
