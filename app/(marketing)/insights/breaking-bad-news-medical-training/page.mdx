SPIKES gives clinicians a structure for breaking bad news, but a six-step protocol becomes useful only when a learner can carry it through a real conversation with emotion, uncertainty, and questions.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What SPIKES organizes

Baile and colleagues described SPIKES as six linked tasks: set up the conversation, assess the patient's perception, ask how much information the patient wants, share knowledge, respond to emotion, and summarize the strategy. The protocol gives a learner a sequence to return to when the conversation becomes hard.

It does not turn the conversation into a script. A parent may interrupt before the diagnosis is complete. A patient may want less detail than the clinician expected. Emotion may need attention before the plan can continue. The learner still has to listen and adapt.

## Practice should expose the missed move

A lecture can explain why a warning shot matters. Practice shows whether the learner actually used one, whether the diagnosis was stated in plain language, and whether the learner paused when emotion entered the conversation.

The feedback should point to the exact moment. "Be more empathic" gives the learner nowhere to start. A transcript-grounded note can show that the learner moved from the diagnosis to treatment before acknowledging the parent's fear, and the next attempt can focus on that behavior.

## Keep live assessment for the high-stakes decision

AI patients can provide repeated voice-based practice before an OSCE, a standardized patient session, or a coached clinical encounter. They do not replace the human judgment required for summative assessment.

That division of labor gives learners more chances to practice while faculty and standardized patients stay focused on the encounters where their presence matters most.
